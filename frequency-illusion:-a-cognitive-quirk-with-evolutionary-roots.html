Frequency Illusion: A Cognitive Quirk with Evolutionary Roots

Fri, August 14, 2026

1. What is it?

The Frequency Illusion is a cognitive bias where, after noticing something for the first time, you suddenly start seeing it everywhere.

It occurs in two distinct stages:

  1. Selective Attention: Your brain subconsciously flags a new concept, word, or object as "important." Because of this, your reticular activating system (RAS) begins to filter environmental stimuli to prioritize that specific thing.
  2. Confirmation Bias: You actively look for that item to prove your new awareness correct. When you inevitably spot it, your brain reinforces the belief that it is "everywhere," while ignoring all the instances where it is not present.

Example: You learn the word "defenestration" (the act of throwing someone out a window). Over the next week, you see it in a novel, hear it in a podcast, and read it in a newspaper. The word didn't go viral; your brain simply tuned into a frequency that was always there.

2. The Biology of Attention: The Reticular Activating System (RAS)

To understand the Illusion, you have to look at the brain's gatekeeper: the RAS. This bundle of nerves at your brainstem sits between your conscious mind (the cortex) and your subconscious sensory input.

  • The Filter: Your senses take in roughly 11 million bits of information per second, but your conscious mind can only process about 50 bits per second.
  • The Bouncer: The RAS acts as a bouncer, deciding which 50 bits get through. It prioritizes stimuli based on danger (loud noises), relevance (your name being called), and novelty (a new concept you just learned).

When you learn a new word, the RAS updates its priority list. It doesn't care if the word is useful; it only cares that you have recently labeled it as important. As a result, it allows that specific data to pass through the filter, creating the illusion of a massive spike in frequency.

3. The Evolutionary Survival Link

At first glance, the Frequency Illusion seems like a useless quirk. Why would evolution favor a system that makes us think we are seeing things more than we actually are?

The answer lies in threat detection and pattern recognition. To a prehistoric human, missing a pattern could mean death, while falsely recognizing a pattern usually just meant wasted energy.

A. The "Berry Patch" Hypothesis

Imagine an ancestral human foraging in the jungle. They eat a strange red berry and get violently ill.

  • Without the Illusion: They would simply avoid that specific berry bush.
  • With the Illusion (Selective Attention): Their brain instantly flags the color red and the shape of that berry as "highly relevant to survival." Over the next few days, the Frequency Illusion kicks in. They start spotting red berries everywhere—in trees, in vines, on the ground.

The brain isn't trying to trick them; it is hyper-priming their visual cortex to identify potential threats. Even if 99% of the red berries are safe, the 1% chance of a fatal mistake forces the brain to over-index on the pattern.

B. Predator vs. Prey Dynamics

For our ancestors, spotting a tiger in the tall grass was a life-or-death moment.

  • The brain is wired to err on the side of caution (better safe than sorry).
  • The Frequency Illusion ensures that after you spot a tiger in a particular region of the savanna, you do not relax. Your RAS keeps your attention locked on stripes, orange colors, and rustling grass.
  • You hallucinate the frequency of tigers to be higher than it actually is, ensuring you remain vigilant. In evolutionary terms, paranoia is a survival advantage when the cost of missing a threat is total annihilation.

C. Social Cohesion and Tribal Language

Humans are tribal pack animals. Language and social bonding were critical for survival.

  • When a shaman or a leader introduces a new concept (e.g., "The river spirits are angry"), the tribe experiences a collective Frequency Illusion. They begin to see "signs" of angry spirits everywhere (thunder, dying fish, bad weather).
  • This shared illusion reinforces tribal unity. Believing in the same patterns—even if they are statistically coincidental—kept the group cohesive, allowing them to cooperate more effectively against external threats.

4. The Modern Disconnect (Why it misfires today)

In the ancestral environment, the Frequency Illusion was highly functional. The "new thing" you noticed was almost always tied to physical survival—food, predators, or social hierarchy.

However, in the modern environment, our RAS is hijacked by low-stakes data:

  • We learn a new slang word and hear it everywhere.
  • We buy a blue car and suddenly see the exact same model on every street.
  • We read about a rare disease and immediately diagnose ourselves with it.

The Mismatch: Our ancient brains cannot differentiate between a "survival-level" pattern (a predator) and a "trivial" pattern (a car model). The RAS treats both with the same evolutionary urgency. This is why the Frequency Illusion can cause anxiety in the modern world—we trick our survival instincts into thinking a new fashion trend or stock market term is a "wolf at the door."

5. Summary Table

Aspect Frequency Illusion Mechanism Evolutionary Survival Purpose
Attention RAS filters to prioritize new data. Allows rapid identification of new food sources or dangers.
Confirmation Brain seeks out the new concept. Builds a robust mental map of the environment.
False Positives Seeing the pattern where it doesn't exist. Better to over-react than under-react. (Survival bias).
Social Spread Groups reinforce the illusion together. Strengthens tribal bonds and shared communication.
Modern Misfire Applied to consumer goods and media. Causes anxiety; no longer tied to physical survival.

Conclusion

The Frequency Illusion is not a bug in our operating system; it is a legacy feature from the Pleistocene era. It is the ghost of our ancestors whispering, "Pay attention to that—it might try to eat you, or it might feed your family."

While it leads to amusing coincidences in daily life, understanding its evolutionary roots helps us recognize when our brain is simply running an outdated survival script—allowing us to consciously override the illusion and say, "It's not everywhere; I'm just looking for it."
